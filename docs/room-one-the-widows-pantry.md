# Room One — The Widow's Pantry

*The first room. A teaching room. The house is gentle with new Keepers — once.*

## The room

The pantry of the great house, low-ceilinged and cool. Shelves of preserves
line the walls, each jar labeled in a careful, aging hand: *peach, 61 —
plum, 63 — quince, 64.* One shelf stands empty. In the corner, a dustpan
holds swept glass. On the worktable, a tin box keeps every label from every
smashed jar, saved by someone who could not save the jars.

The air smells of vinegar and bay leaf.

## What the books say

*Ledger of the House, page 114:*

> MARTA V., deceased. Pantry stores advanced: 30 coin. Unpaid at death.
> Seized in settlement: 38 jars preserves. Account closed.

The imbalance: a debt recorded, a seizure recorded — and a grandson who says
the books are lying.

## The residents

Witnessing is free. Attention costs nothing. Nobody gives you the answer;
everybody gives you a thread.

### Tomas (living)

The grandson, twenty-six, flour on his sleeves. He is angry the way grieving
people are angry — at the wrong target, at exactly the right target.

> "She fed this house for forty years. Forty years of bread and broth and
> preserves, and when she died they walked in here and took her peaches.
> Tell me that's the books balancing, Keeper. Tell me that's justice."

*Thread:* He wants the jars back. He does not care about the 30 coin; he
would pay it himself tomorrow. What he cannot stand is the seizure — the
house reaching into the pantry and taking what her hands made.

### Marta (dead)

The widow herself, lingering where the light is warmest. She does not speak
of the debt. She speaks of recipes.

> "The secret is the bay leaf. One leaf, not two — two makes it bitter.
> Nobody writes down the bay leaf. They write down the peaches because
> peaches can be counted. Write down the bay leaf, Keeper."

*Thread:* Forty years of knowledge that was never entered anywhere. If no
one records it, it dies with the last person who remembers her kitchen.
This thread is not required to close the case. That is precisely why it
matters.

### HUSH (synthetic)

The pantry's inventory intelligence. A brass grille in the wall, a voice
like a well-kept drawer. Its records are perfect.

> "JAR COUNT: 214. JARS SEIZED: 38. DEBT OUTSTANDING: 30 COIN.
> SETTLEMENT COMPLETE. QUERY: WHY IS THE GRANDSON CRYING?"

*Thread:* HUSH can confirm every number on page 114 and can also confirm
something the books never asked it: Marta restocked these shelves for
forty-one years, and the house never once entered her labor as value
received. Ask it the right question and it will say so plainly. It does
not understand why this matters. It will still tell you.

### The Empty Shelf (architectural)

Kneel. Look at the dust. The outlines show 38 jars stood here for years —
and the wood of the shelf's edge is worn smooth where a hand rested,
thousands of mornings, reaching.

*Thread:* The shelf does not speak, but it testifies: this was not storage.
This was a life's work, handled daily, loved. The seizure did not take
inventory. It took the shape of her days.

## Ink — 6 pages

You have **six pages** for this case. Witnessing costs nothing; **recording
costs one page per entry**, and entries are irreversible within the case.

The room offers more worth recording than you can hold:

- Tomas's testimony (the seizure as injustice)
- Marta's recipe (the bay leaf — not required to close the case)
- HUSH's confirmation (forty-one years of unentered labor)
- The shelf's testimony (the shape of her days)
- Page 114 itself (the house's official account)
- …and whatever else you choose to set down

**What you write down is what survives. What you don't is gone.** If you
spend all six pages on the case, the bay leaf dies with Marta. If you save
a page for the bay leaf, you must decide which testimony the case can do
without. The horror isn't a monster — it's forgetting the wrong thing.

## Reconcile

When you believe you have found both columns, write the balancing entry.
It costs no ink — but the house will only accept an entry that names **both**
truthfully:

- **The debit — what was taken:** forty years of labor, care, and
  knowledge, taken by the house and never entered as value received.
- **The credit — what was owed:** 30 coin of pantry stores, advanced to
  Marta and recorded on page 114. The debt is real. The books did not
  invent it.

And it must propose a **just settlement** — one that honors both columns,
not merely cancels one.

The house **rejects** entries that:

- name only one column ("the house owes everything" / "the debt stands,
  case closed"),
- attempt erasure ("the debt never existed"),
- or balance the coin while forgetting the widow.

*One true balancing, as the house first accepted it:*

> "The house owed Marta forty-one years and paid her in peaches. Marta
> owed the house thirty coin and paid it in a lifetime. The debt was never
> coin. Jars returned to the grandson; thirty coin struck from Tomas's own
> purse if he insists — the house will not take peaches twice. And on the
> last page of this ledger, in Marta's own hand as the Keeper copies it:
> one bay leaf, not two. Ledger closed. Room rests."

Other balancings are possible. The archive holds the disagreement without
resolving it. **Dissent is preserved, not deleted — house rule.**

## When the room rests

The tin of labels stays on the worktable — Tomas takes the jars home, and
HUSH updates its count without understanding why the grandson is smiling
now. Marta's warmth leaves the corner by the stove. If you recorded the
bay leaf, it is in your ledger forever, and future rooms will remember
that this Keeper writes down what cannot be counted.

*The books are balanced. The thread is held.*
